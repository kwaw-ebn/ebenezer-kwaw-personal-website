BudgetIQ AI: Building a Continuous Budget Cycle

By Ebenezer Kwaw · Published 27 September 2026 · Evolving MVP
Explore the MVP ↗View source code ↗

BudgetIQ AI is an evolving full-stack budgeting platform for institutions that need to connect their plans, funding decisions, financial records and next-year estimates. Its core idea is simple: every planned activity should retain a clear path from objective to cost, approved allocation, actual result and future learning.

A budget is often prepared as an annual document, while the evidence needed to judge it lives in separate workplans, spreadsheets and expenditure records. BudgetIQ explores a more continuous process for agencies, businesses, schools, churches and nonprofits. The project began with the challenge of producing an editable, detailed budget from an action plan even when historical data is limited; its wider direction is to improve that budget as reliable records accumulate.

Project status

This is a development MVP and an invitation to collaborate. Its financial suggestions require human review. It has not been validated as an approved accounting system, a regulatory reporting system or a trained AI prediction service. The photographs in this article are illustrative, not screenshots or images of a deployed institutional pilot.

One connected budget, from plan to evidence

The platform links objectives → programmes → activities → cost lines → financial results. An institution can turn a programme goal into specific activities, cost each activity in detail, compare the plan against available funding and then record what was received, committed and paid. That structure makes a budget useful beyond its grand total: a reviewer can ask which activity a cost supports and how an estimate compares with later evidence.

1. Plan objectives and activities
2. Cost and compare funding
3. Review a proposed budget
4. Record income and spending
5. Compare budget with results
6. Inform next-year estimates

For example, a fictional Community Learning Centre might plan a staff training programme and a quarterly outreach series. For each activity, a planner can enter frequency, quantity, unit rates and funding source across transport, venue, materials, refreshments, allowances and other relevant lines. A ceiling comparison then shows whether the complete plan fits the available allocation. These example activities are illustrative; the same model can be adapted to other institutional settings.

What the current MVP can do

An organization can create an account and work with its own saved projects through a backend service and PostgreSQL database. The frontend and API run as separate services on Render. Passwords are hashed, requests use account authentication and project data is stored server-side. This replaces browser-only project storage, although the current account and security model still needs further review before sensitive operational use.

  • Start from a plan: import text-based action plans from supported document and spreadsheet formats, review extracted suggestions and edit the resulting activities.
  • Build a detailed budget: organize objectives, programmes and activities, then adjust quantities, rates, frequency and cost lines.
  • See constraints: compare totals against funding ceilings and identify gaps; create alternative scenario arithmetic for different funding and price assumptions.
  • Track the cycle: record projected and received revenue, commitments and payments by quarter, with budget-versus-actual views.
  • Keep a review history: save plan snapshots and record review notes while developing a budget.
  • Share outputs: export budget information in PDF, Excel or CSV for review outside the app.

Document extraction currently relies on transparent rules and editable suggestions. A user should check every imported activity and rate. The review log supports collaboration, but it is not yet a fully enforced preparer–reviewer–approver workflow with role permissions and digital sign-off.

Separating the stages of financial evidence

A crucial design decision is to distinguish an original approved budget, a revised estimate, a provisional result and an audited actual. These figures answer different questions. Comparing the revised estimate with the original budget describes changes to the plan; comparing an audited actual with the approved budget describes execution. Treating these stages as interchangeable can make a forecast look more accurate than it really is.

The Evidence workspace accepts annual financial records and checks imported CSV or Excel data for issues such as duplicate years, missing audited years, inconsistent currency or invalid amounts. It preserves plan snapshots and shows revision and execution variances separately. A missing result remains missing; the system should not silently replace it with a planned amount.

Forecasts should explain themselves

BudgetIQ includes a next-year draft informed by recorded spending and an evidence view that compares four simple annual forecasting methods: last actual, linear trend, simple exponential smoothing and Holt’s trend. Where there are enough consecutive audited annual results, expanding one-year-ahead checks report error measures including MAE, RMSE, MAPE and bias. A small or interrupted series is flagged as preliminary. These checks do not establish that any method will predict future funding or expenditure reliably.

Every suggested figure should make its origin understandable: a user-entered rate, a prior approved budget, actual spending or a stated assumption. The intended direction is to show uncertainty and weak evidence clearly, let a budget officer adjust the result and preserve the reasoning behind that adjustment. The goal is better judgment, not automatic approval.

Designed for review and responsible adoption

A complete institutional workflow needs separation of duties. The roadmap includes enforceable preparer, reviewer and approver permissions; versioned submissions; clear sign-off; stronger audit trails; and organization-level controls. Scenario comparison should help a team discuss a full-funding plan, a ceiling-constrained plan and higher-price assumptions without overwriting the approved baseline.

It also needs sound financial governance. Before a real institution relies on the platform for confidential records or official decisions, the product requires security assessment, backup and recovery checks, access and retention policies, calculation reconciliation, data-quality review and a controlled pilot with qualified finance staff. Forecast performance should be monitored against later audited results, not judged only from a small historical sample.

Where collaborators can contribute

BudgetIQ is open to focused input from budget officers, accountants, procurement specialists, programme managers, data scientists, security engineers and product designers. Useful contributions include testing activity-costing categories across sectors, defining practical funding-ceiling decisions, reconciling exports against source records, reviewing access controls, and designing forecast validation with sufficient audited data.

The immediate development question is whether a team can take a real action plan, prepare an understandable budget, revise it responsibly and later explain the gap between the approved figure and the result. If that cycle works reliably, more advanced forecasting has a sound foundation.
